The Press Files

On October 1st, the IDF published a searchable database of 170 dead "journalists" — each entry with a name, a role, and a terror group's ID number. Days earlier, the Committee to Protect Journalists had quietly deleted 19 names from its own casualty list. This is the story of who was counting.

OCTOBER 7, 2026 · 7 MIN READ

The blue press vest is the most trusted garment in war reporting. It says: don't shoot — I'm here to witness. On October 1st, the Israel Defense Forces published an online database arguing that in Gaza, 170 of those vests belonged to men who also carried rifles. It named them, one by one, with their military ID numbers.

The release was titled "The Press Files." What makes it difficult to dismiss is not who published it — it's who confirmed parts of it before the IDF said a word.

I. The database

"Following extensive research, the IDF is now revealing that 170 Gazans killed during the war who were presented as journalists and media personnel were in fact serving as terrorists for terror organizations in the Strip, foremost among them Hamas and Palestinian Islamic Jihad." That is the army's statement, carried by the Times of Israel on October 1st.

The breakdown, as published: 118 of the 170 were active in Hamas's military wing, 49 operated in Palestinian Islamic Jihad, two belonged to the Popular Resistance Committees, one to the Mujahideen Brigades. Seventeen of the people on the list, the army says, crossed into Israeli territory in the October 7th attack itself.

Some of the names, the IDF says, also worked for international outlets — Reuters, AP, CNN, BBC, AFP, Le Monde, Anadolu, ABC Australia. Each of the 170 entries on the IDF's dedicated website carries the person's name and role, alongside their civil and military ID numbers. The military says it is publishing "verified evidence, including visual material and documents produced by the terror organizations, some of which were found in the field."

The first ten files were released with paired photographs: the men as journalists, and the men as operatives. Among them: a Reuters photographer; a Gaza radio station's director-general; a man whose name is engraved at the Reporters Without Borders memorial in France; and a man whose death was included in a complaint against Israel at the International Criminal Court.

"Journalists are expected to report the unbiased truth. Yet these individuals concealed a fundamental truth about themselves."

IDF statement, October 1, 2026

II. The audit

Here is the part that doesn't come from Israel. Days before the IDF published anything, the Committee to Protect Journalists — the New York-based organization whose casualty database is quoted in newsrooms worldwide — revised its own Gaza list and removed 19 people it had previously classified as journalists, after determining they were militants or had participated in combat.

The trigger, according to the reporting, wasn't Israeli intelligence. It was the terror groups' own obituaries: Hamas and Palestinian Islamic Jihad published death notices identifying as combatants some individuals who had appeared in CPJ's database as journalists. CPJ examined 229 deaths documented between October 2023 and August 2026.

CPJ's chief executive, Jodie Ginsberg, said the organization "condemns in no uncertain terms the misrepresentation of combatants as journalists or media workers" — noting, pointedly, that the practice endangers genuine journalists.

Read that again slowly. The organization that certifies who counts as a slain journalist looked at its own list — and started deleting.

III. The files

Statistics persuade analysts. Files persuade everyone else. Three entries from the record:

Mohammed Mahmoud Abdel Aziz Abu Sharia. His name is engraved at the Reporters Without Borders memorial in France — a monument to murdered journalists. The IDF's file identifies him as a terror operative. Somewhere in France, stone still says otherwise.

Amro Nahid Abdel Rahman Abu Odeh. A photographer for Reuters. Also, according to the IDF's file, something else entirely.

Anas al-Sharif — pictured above, in the vest, holding the Al Jazeera microphone. The IDF says he had been a Hamas member since 2013, and killed him near Shifa Hospital in August 2025. Hassan Aslih worked as a stringer for AP and CNN, filmed himself embedded with Hamas fighters inside Israel on October 7th, and was photographed kissing Yahya Sinwar before the war; the IDF identifies him as a military-media operative in Hamas's Khan Younis Brigade. He was killed in May 2025.

These are individual claims, each carrying the IDF's evidence with it — documents, the army says, produced by the terror organizations themselves. They can be checked, and they should be.

IV. The study

Before the dossier, there was the ledger. In December, Israel's Meir Amit Intelligence and Terrorism Information Center published a 140-page study examining, name by name, 266 Palestinians identified as journalists or media workers killed in Gaza between October 7th, 2023 and November 30th, 2025. Its conclusion: roughly 60 percent — 157 of the 266 — were members of or affiliated with terrorist organizations, primarily Hamas and Palestinian Islamic Jihad. 104 were linked to Hamas, 45 to Islamic Jihad.

The methodology is worth noting because it is the opposite of a press release: researchers worked through Arabic-language social media, Telegram channels, mourning notices, funeral footage, and the organizations' own statements and claims of responsibility. In many cases, the evidence that a "journalist" was a fighter came from the fighters' side of the family — a martyrdom poster here, a brigade's condolence notice there.

"[We] condemn in no uncertain terms the misrepresentation of combatants as journalists or media workers."

Jodie Ginsberg, CEO, Committee to Protect Journalists

V. What this doesn't prove

Two honest caveats, because the file demands them.

First: the 170 are the IDF's claims, from military investigations — and at least one major outlet, Al Jazeera, calls the underlying documents fabricated. Treat the dossier as evidence presented, not verdict delivered. Some of it will hold up; some of it may not.

Second: none of this means every name on every casualty list is false, or that no genuine journalists died in Gaza. Some certainly did, and their deaths deserve the same grief as anyone's.

What it does mean is narrower — and harder to wave away. The lists themselves, the ones quoted in headlines and UN statements and ICC filings, were counting Hamas and Islamic Jihad operatives as journalists. Not as a hostile allegation. As the counters' own corrected admission.

The vest

For three years, a number did enormous work in the world's argument about this war: the number of journalists Israel had killed. It anchored editorials, resolutions, award speeches. It turns out almost nobody was auditing the list — until the list started auditing itself.

The vest said: don't shoot, I'm here to witness. The question the Press Files leaves behind is simpler than who shot whom. It is who was wearing it — and who was doing the counting.
