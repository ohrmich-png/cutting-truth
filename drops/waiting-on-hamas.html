Waiting on Hamas

Brett McGurk was the Biden administration's negotiator in every hostage round for fifteen months. Across two interviews this weekend — a long-form podcast and CBS's Face the Nation — his on-the-record account of why every deal collapsed, and who collapsed them.

OCTOBER 6, 2026 · 9 MIN READ

For fifteen months, the most widely repeated explanation for why the hostages weren't coming home was that Israel wouldn't make a deal — that Netanyahu was sabotaging negotiations, that Washington just needed to squeeze Jerusalem harder. Now the man whose job it was to make the deal has spoken on the record, in detail, deal by deal. And his account points the other way.

Brett McGurk served as President Biden's White House Coordinator for the Middle East and North Africa. He was the lead American negotiator in every round of hostage and ceasefire talks in 2023, 2024, and January 2025 — across four presidencies' worth of national security experience. This weekend, in his first long-form on-the-record interview on the negotiations (with Dan Senor's Call Me Back podcast, tied to his new book Brink), he described what happened inside the rooms. What follows is drawn from his words.

I. "Complete bullshit"

Start with the claim you'll still hear: that in the days after October 7th, Hamas signaled it was prepared to simply release the hostages, and Israel refused. McGurk's verdict is blunt.

Hamas's core demand, he says, never changed from the beginning: all Palestinian prisoners, a permanent ceasefire — the October 6th status quo — and Hamas remaining in power, reconstituted on Israel's border. "No Israeli government was ever going to accept that," he notes. Israel's position, unanimous across its leadership, was that Hamas could not remain in power in Gaza when this ended. The two positions were, in his word, irreconcilable — and bridging them was the work of fifteen months.

"The idea that Hamas was just ready to release all the hostages — that's complete bullshit."

Brett McGurk, Biden's lead hostage negotiator

II. November: the deal Hamas broke

The first deal — late November 2023, all women and children — is the one most often cited as proof negotiations could work. McGurk's telling adds two details the retellings usually omit.

First: it was Netanyahu who pushed for more. In Israel's defense headquarters, with Hamas offering a guaranteed 20 hostages on the table, McGurk floated 30. Netanyahu pulled him aside: "Brett, I need this deal. Help me get it." Then: "No, no, no — demand fifty." They got fifty. A hundred hostages came out over eight days of ceasefire.

Second: it was Hamas that broke it. On day eight, with the guns silent and the remaining names on the list being the younger women — most taken from the Nova festival or their homes — Hamas sent a message: it couldn't "find" the younger girls, so let's skip to the older men and keep the ceasefire. Israel's answer was unanimous: no. As McGurk puts it, skipping the women would have been "signing their death sentence" — they would never have come out. The war restarted that night.

Why did Hamas hold the young women back? McGurk is direct: they were the most valuable hostages "for all the worst reasons" — and there was a shared assessment at the time that Hamas feared what the women would tell the world once released. Three of those women — Romi, Doron, and Emily — should have come out in November. They came out the day before the January 2025 inauguration, fourteen months later.

McGurk's conclusion about Hamas's strategy: "I think Hamas believed, particularly Yahya Sinwar, that this war would end with Hamas holding a number of hostages. Meaning indefinitely."

III. The pressure myth

The second persistent claim: that Washington could have ended the war and freed the hostages by pressuring — or cutting off — Israel. McGurk, who argued against the cutoff inside the room, says the record shows the opposite.

When the U.S. took its most public step in that direction — abstaining on a UN Security Council resolution for a Ramadan ceasefire not tied to the hostages — it produced no ceasefire. Instead, Hamas leader Ismail Haniyeh traveled to Tehran, praised the move, and doubled down on Hamas's demands. "A very tangible example," McGurk says, "of performative diplomacy producing no outcome whatsoever. In fact, producing negative outcomes."

The same applied to the tortured public debate over Rafah. McGurk says the public ultimatums didn't change Israel's operation — private consultations did — but they "definitely played back into the hostage negotiation." Yahya Sinwar, the man holding the literal keys to the hostages, was in Rafah at the time, consuming media. The prospect of a U.S.-Israel breach was exactly what he wanted to see.

And the cutoff theory itself? "Israel has one of the largest arms industries in the world," McGurk notes, "so the idea that had we made draconian cuts in military assistance it would've stopped the war — it's just not well-founded." Congress, meanwhile, voted 366 to 58 for a $26 billion aid package that April. The consensus for a cutoff that the retellings imagine never existed.

"Just cut off Israel and somehow the war will stop, the hostages will come out… it doesn't add up."

Brett McGurk

Asked directly on Face the Nation whether stopping American support for Israel would end the crisis, McGurk didn't hedge: "That is not going to end the crisis. It's going to make it worse."

IV. "Just say yes"

Then came the two American proposals of 2024 — and the clearest test of who was blocking a deal.

May 31, 2024

Biden lays out a detailed deal. Israel says yes. The UN Security Council endorses it unanimously. Hamas doesn't answer for five weeks — then claims to "accept" with poison pills attached.

August 16, 2024

A new proposal goes ~80% of the way toward Hamas, endorsed publicly by Qatar's emir and Egypt's president. Days of silence follow. Then: six hostages found murdered in a Rafah tunnel.

December 2024

Netanyahu agrees: if Hamas produces the list of 33 hostages, it's a deal. Hamas answers that only 12 of the 33 are alive.

January 2025

Sinwar dead, Hezbollah decoupled, McGurk and Trump's envoy Witkoff working as one team. Netanyahu agrees once he has the full list of 33 names. Deal.

On the May proposal, McGurk is explicit: "If you want this war to end, on June 1st, right after that speech, all pressure should be on Hamas. Just say yes." Instead, Hezbollah launched its largest attack of the war, Iran's supreme leader praised October 7th, and Hamas sat silent.

The August answer was the war's darkest moment. McGurk was at lunch with his family, checking with the Qataris and Egyptians, expecting good news — "we think we're gonna get an answer soon." Instead, CIA Director Bill Burns called: the IDF had found six hostages — Hersh Goldberg-Polin, Eden Yerushalmi, Carmel Gat, Ori Danino, Alex Lobanov, Almog Sarusi — executed in a tunnel under Rafah. McGurk had been carrying a list of names for the deal. Hersh and Carmel were on it; both should have come out in November.

What did Hamas do then? "Did they say, 'this was a tragic mistake,' or a guard panicked or something? No. They released videos of the dead — and the videos came out, like, when the funerals were going on." Propaganda videos, McGurk says, "designed to torture Israelis."

His conclusion, delivered to Biden around the table days later: "We're not gonna get a hostage deal until Yahya Sinwar is dead. It's completely irreconcilable."

There was one more near-miss, revealed in the Face the Nation interview. In December 2024, McGurk got Netanyahu to agree: if Hamas produced the list of 33 hostages — the names Israel had, the people coming out — it was a deal. Hamas's answer: of the 33, only 12 would be alive. McGurk sat with the Qatari prime minister holding the list — Keith Siegel, an American, on it — and faced the same moral math as that November midnight: take the 12 and stop the war, or hold out for the full list, abandoning the rest? "All we needed at that moment to stop the war," he says, "was an agreement on the list of hostages." Hamas wouldn't give it. Weeks later, after New Year's, they produced the full list — then piled new demands on the table.

V. What finally worked

What changed wasn't pressure on Israel. It was the strategic environment: Israel went into Lebanon, Nasrallah was killed, Sinwar was killed on October 16th, Hezbollah cut its own deal — "that cuts the legs out from under Hamas." And then the rarest thing in Washington: the outgoing and incoming teams worked as one. McGurk called Jared Kushner after the election; Steve Witkoff, Trump's envoy, told him "I don't give a shit about politics, I just wanna save lives." They spoke every day. In December, Netanyahu said he was ready if he got the full list — 33 names. In January, the deal closed.

McGurk gives credit both ways: "I give Trump and Steve full credit, and I also give Biden and our team full credit." The threat that concentrated minds, he acknowledges, was Trump's: if the hostages weren't home by Inauguration Day, "all hell will break loose." Nobody knew what it meant. It worked.

VI. The rest of the record

The Face the Nation interview adds several entries to the record that rarely survive the retellings.

The hospital. In the war's second week, headlines around the world reported that an Israeli airstrike had killed 500 Palestinians at Al-Ahli Hospital. The blast also killed a planned Amman summit — Jordan's king withdrew the invitation — where Biden was to meet Arab leaders about civilian protection. McGurk, on the record: the headline "turned out not to be true."

The border that stayed shut. With Gaza's civilians trapped, McGurk called Egypt's intelligence chief and asked for Gazans to be allowed into Sinai temporarily. "Answer is absolutely not." When Israel later designated Mawasi as a safe zone and asked for help setting it up, the UN refused to cooperate — it would not, McGurk says, "even be a part of encouraging people to move or to setting up safe areas," on the grounds it might look like forced displacement.

The tunnels. Hamas spent twenty years building military infrastructure under Gaza. Civilians, McGurk notes, had nowhere to go — "the underground facilities were only for Hamas, they made that very clear." He contrasts it with Mosul, where a million civilians were evacuated from the ISIS fight: in Gaza, there was nowhere for people to go, and no one willing to open a door.

What was knocked off the table

There is a refrain that runs through McGurk's account, almost a drumbeat: "once again, you're waiting on Hamas." Days go by. Another day goes by. The mediators say an answer is coming. And then — silence, a poison pill, a propaganda video, six bodies in a tunnel. For fifteen months, the explanation most of the world heard was that the obstacle sat in Jerusalem. The man who sat in every negotiating room says the obstacle sat in Gaza — and in the end, in a tunnel in Rafah, holding the keys.

Perhaps the most striking revelation sits at the very beginning of the story. On October 6th, 2023, McGurk shook hands with Saudi Princess Reema on a normalization package — Israel-Saudi recognition, a Saudi civilian nuclear program, and, crucially, "a credible pathway to Palestinian state determination and statehood," language McGurk says would later appear in a UN Security Council resolution. A senior Saudi official had just visited Ramallah for the first time since 1967. Israeli officials were in Riyadh. Hours later, October 7th knocked it all off the agenda.

Was that the point? McGurk is careful — "we never saw anything to tie a direct connection" — but he adds: "I would not be surprised if one of the motivations on October 7th was to knock this agenda off the table."

Consider what that means. The attack that its celebrants frame as resistance may have been aimed, among other things, at destroying the most credible pathway to Palestinian statehood in half a century. And the fifteen months that followed — every collapsed deal, every "just say yes" that went unanswered — were, in the account of the man in the room, fifteen months of waiting on Hamas.

The record is there for anyone who wants it.
