The Photos Were
From February

Angelina Jolie showed the world Gaza from space. The images were eight months old — and the story skipped its first chapter.

OCTOBER 6, 2026 · 5 MIN READ

Over the weekend, Angelina Jolie did what only a handful of people on earth can do: she made satellite imagery go viral. The actress and former UN refugee agency envoy posted before-and-after Google Earth views of Rafah, Khan Younis and Gaza City to Instagram — intact streets on one side, rubble on the other — with a single instruction: "You can now open Google Earth and see what was done to Gaza."

Within eleven hours, more than 800,000 people had liked it; the count approached a million. Within days, outlets from Ankara to Kuala Lumpur were carrying the images. It was, by any measure, one of the most-seen posts about Gaza ever published.

There was just one problem with the pictures. They weren't new.

I. The dates

The images, as presented, showed Gaza as it is now. According to the investigative outlet Bellingcat, they were captured in February — by the imaging company Airbus — roughly eight months before Jolie posted them. Google itself told Newsweek that its most recent satellite imagery of the Strip dates to June of this year.

Eight months is a long time in a war. Between February and October, ceasefires were declared and broken, negotiations opened and collapsed, hostages came home and the dead were buried. None of that is visible from orbit — which is precisely the point. A satellite image is a fact about a moment. Presented without its date, it becomes a fact about whenever you want it to be.

II. The missing beginning

Jolie's post cited UN satellite analysis: 82 percent of Gaza's structures damaged as of mid-June, nearly two million people needing shelter before winter. She wrote of neighborhoods reduced to rubble, of hospitals and schools, of aid facing "restrictions and delays." Taken on its own terms, it was a humanitarian appeal — and a powerful one.

But read the post as published — in every passage quoted across the reporting — and something is absent. There is no mention of October 7th. No mention of Hamas. No mention of why there is a war in Gaza at all, or what began it, or what would end it.

This is not a small omission. It is the story's first chapter. A before-and-after image has a simple grammar: this is what was, this is what is. What it cannot show — what no satellite can show — is why. And when the "why" is removed, the viewer is left with only one possible reading: that the rubble simply appeared, that destruction is something "done to" Gaza by no one in particular, for no reason at all.

What the satellite can't show

Jolie wrote one true sentence in her post: "Google Earth shows the physical destruction with extraordinary clarity. What it cannot show is what it means to live inside it."

She was right about the clarity. The images are brutally clear. What they cannot show is their own date — a satellite doesn't timestamp its grief. And they cannot show the morning of October 7, 2023, when everything in those photographs began: the murder of some 1,200 people in their homes, at a music festival, on the roadside.

The photos were from February. The story started long before. Anyone who wants you to look at the rubble without looking at the beginning is not showing you the full picture — no matter how high the satellite flies.
