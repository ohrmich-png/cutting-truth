The List-Keeper

BBC Arabic's list of “journalists” killed in Gaza — the one found to include Hamas operatives who took part in October 7th — was compiled by Layla Bashar al-Kloub. Her decade of social media posts calls the killers of Jews “martyrs.” The British Council gave her an award.

OCTOBER 10, 2026 · 5 MIN READ

The BBC has a list. It is a list of “journalists” killed in Gaza, published on the BBC Arabic website, updated as recently as August 2025, and quoted around the world as fact. Last month, an investigation found that 53 of the names on it had links to Hamas or other armed groups — and that some had taken part in the October 7th attacks themselves.

This week, the Telegraph revealed who compiled the list: Layla Bashar al-Kloub, a BBC Arabic reporter. And it revealed what she has been writing on social media for a decade: tributes to killers of Jews, whom she calls “martyrs.”

I. The list

BBC Arabic first published its casualty list of media workers in 2023 and kept updating it through the war. The premise was simple and powerful: Israel was killing journalists. The list gave the claim a number, and the number gave it authority.

Then the list was audited. In September, the Telegraph reported that 53 people the BBC had described as “journalists” had links to Hamas or other armed groups — some alleged to have participated in the October 7th massacres. Even the Committee to Protect Journalists, whose own data the BBC leaned on, quietly removed twelve names from its casualty list after concluding they were “not journalists or media workers” or had “participated in combat.” One of them: Abdullah Darwish, described by BBC Arabic as a cameraman for Al-Aqsa TV — who had joined the October 7th attacks as a member of Hamas's Al-Qassam Brigades and was killed in a targeted strike that December.

The list, in other words, was never a neutral fact. It was a curation. And now we know the curator.

II. The compiler

According to the Telegraph's reporting, highlighted by the media-monitoring group CAMERA, al-Kloub's social media record reads like a decade-long audition for the other side:

In 2014, she wrote to an Israeli citizen that “before 1948 it was Palestine” — before, in her words, the terrorists came. In 2016, she tweeted that “the Zionist entity” recognizes no international law: “They are the terrorists, not us.” In 2017, she posted that the land “from the river to the sea” was “Arab, Arab, Arab” — a formula that leaves no room for Israel to exist.

In 2018 and 2019, she described Ahmad Jarrar and Omar Abu Leila as “martyrs.” Both men had murdered Jewish civilians before being killed themselves. And in 2021, she publicly embraced Muna Hawwa, an Al Jazeera presenter who had been disciplined by her own channel after sharing a video asking how true the Holocaust was — writing to her, in the Telegraph's account, words of warmth and encouragement for her “exquisite journalism.”

This is the person the BBC entrusted to decide, name by name, who counts as a journalist in Gaza.

III. The award

In April 2025, the British Council gave al-Kloub an award for being part of the BBC Arabic team covering the Gaza war. A CAMERA spokesman put it plainly: she should not have been allowed to cover the subject for the BBC at all, let alone be decorated for it.

The BBC's response, in full, was the standard one. The list was “based on the information available at the time it was published”; the broadcaster is “looking at those included in the piece” in light of the CPJ's update. On al-Kloub herself: allegations of social media breaches are taken “very seriously,” urgent action was taken to investigate — but “we do not comment on individual staff matters.” She was approached for comment and did not respond.

Note what the BBC would not say: whether she is staff or freelance, or how many times she has appeared. The institution that publishes her list will not describe her role in it.

IV. The meaning

Al-Kloub is not the first. She is the fourth name in a series the Telegraph has been building for over a year: Samer Elzaenen, who wrote that Jews should be burned “as Hitler did” and appeared on BBC Arabic 244 times; Ahmed Qannan, who cheered on the cutting of throats and appeared 217 times; Ahmed Alagha, who called Jews “devils” and appeared 522 times — the figures come from the BBC's own internal review.

Each time, the defense is identical: not staff, just freelancers; not reporters, just eyewitnesses; not aware of the social media history. Each time, the pattern holds: the BBC Arabic operation does not seem to vet the people it hands a microphone to — and when the record surfaces, it will not say what it knew or when.

But al-Kloub's case is worse than a bad booking. She was not a guest reading a line. She built the dataset — the list that turned Hamas operatives into “journalists” in the world's press. A list is only as honest as the person who writes it. The BBC's was written by someone who calls the murderers of Jews martyrs.

The byline

Every news organization makes mistakes. The honest ones correct the record and explain how the mistake happened. The BBC's move, each time, is to shrink the person: not staff, not a reporter, just a contributor. As if the byline were the problem, and not the judgment that put her in charge of the list.

The list is still the story. But now it has an author — and she has a record. Read both together, and the “journalists” of Gaza start to look like exactly what an independent audit found: some reporters, some combatants, and one very committed editor.
