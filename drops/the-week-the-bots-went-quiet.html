The Week the Bots Went Quiet

Ukraine knocked out three Yandex data centers — and bot-driven “Free Palestine” content fell off a cliff. The Kremlin doesn't care about Palestine. It amplifies everything divisive. Its instruments never knew.

OCTOBER 13, 2026 · 5 MIN READ

For four days in October, some of the loudest voices on the internet went quiet. Between October 8th and 11th, Ukrainian drones knocked out three Yandex data centers inside Russia, taking more than 80 Russian cloud services offline. In the same week, people in America, Germany, France, and Britain noticed something strange: the pro-Trump cheerleading thinned out. The AfD boosting faded. The anti-Ukraine pile-ons stopped. And “Free Palestine” — one of the most posted phrases on X — dropped sharply.

The cartoon above was posted as a joke. The joke, it turns out, had a body count of servers.

I. The strikes

The targets were real and confirmed. On October 8th, drones hit the Yandex data center at Sasovo in the Ryazan region — a 12-megawatt site holding two of Yandex's three AI supercomputers. Yandex itself said it could not confirm the hardware would ever run again. On October 9th, the Kaluga campus — the largest, 3,800+ racks — was partly disabled. On October 11th, the Vladimir site was completely shut down, and Yandex Cloud went down with it: more than 80 services offline, from cloud computing to the YandexGPT AI model. NetBlocks measured Yandex Cloud traffic at about 30 percent of normal. Across Russia, people lost taxis, navigation, and banking apps.

Russia started this exchange. Since late September it had struck at least eight major data centers in Kyiv — calling the blackouts a “forced digital detox.” President Zelenskyy described Ukraine's answer as symmetrical: they struck our data centers, so we responded.

II. The quiet

What happened next was reported independently, in different countries, on different platforms, by people with no connection to each other:

Garry Kasparov, on X: the Russian troll bots in his feed “dropped dramatically.” Le Parisien, in France: observers reported fewer right-wing reactions on X. Czech MEP Tomáš Zdechovský: activity around Germany's AfD fell sharply — “so much talk about German patriotism… then the connection from Russia goes down.” The Maltese Herald: less pro-MAGA content, less pro-AfD content, less pro-Palestine content, fewer fake stories about the French presidential election. The independent outlet Malcontent News ran its own tests — posting keyword bait on X and Facebook — and found hostile bot replies to MH-17 and Bucha posts down more than 90 percent, with “Free Palestine” dropping sharply.

The most vivid account came from artist Kelli Klymenko, with 286,000 followers: a pro-Ukraine post of hers usually draws 50 to 75 hostile accounts. That week it drew three.

The topics that went quiet have nothing in common — except that Kremlin networks push all of them.

III. The machine

This is not speculation about who dislikes whom. It is infrastructure. Meta's own Adversarial Threat Report names Russia as the single most frequent source of the covert influence operations it disrupts — 53 networks, against 41 for Iran and 14 for China. One of them, Doppelganger, fakes entire news brands and has splintered into offshoots aimed at France, Germany, Hungary, Armenia, Moldova, and the US.

And here is the technical detail that makes the week's events legible: where a fake account's replies are written by a language model, they are written on someone's servers. The YandexGPT API — Russia's domestic AI model — was on the list of dead services. An operation that writes its comments through an AI API goes silent when that API does. Either that, or the farms' tools, proxies, and connections sat on Yandex Cloud. Either way, the crowd of “concerned” strangers turned out to be running on Russian infrastructure.

A note of honesty, as always: this is a four-day correlation, reported mostly by smaller outlets, and The Verge has urged caution about over-reading the traffic charts. Correlation is not proof. But the witnesses are named, the outages are confirmed, and the pattern — every divisive topic quiet at once — is exactly what a bot-dependent operation looks like when its servers die.

IV. The meaning

Now the uncomfortable part — for the activists, not for us. The Kremlin does not care about Palestine. It does not care about MAGA, or the AfD, or electric cars either. It cares about chaos, and it will amplify anyone who produces it: the far right on Monday, “Free Palestine” on Tuesday, whichever cracks the West further on Wednesday.

Which means the Western pro-Palestinian movement has a question to sit with. When you posted, marched, and chanted — how much of the wave you were riding was real, and how much was manufactured in a data center in Vladimir? Nobody is saying every voice was fake. But the week the servers died showed the ratio more honestly than any debate could: some of the loudest “grassroots” outrage on earth was running on Russian hardware.

They were not allies of Russia. They were its instruments — and most of them never knew.

The instruments

There is a longer history here — the KGB's decades-long cultivation of the Palestinian cause as a propaganda weapon, the Soviet fingerprints on the “Zionism is racism” resolution, the whole machinery. That is a story for its own drop, told one piece at a time.

For now, the lesson of the quiet week is simpler. If your movement's volume depends on someone else's servers, it is worth asking who owns the servers — and what they are buying with your voice.
