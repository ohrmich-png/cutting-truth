Zionism Is Racism:
Examining the Claim

The Green Party of England and Wales just made it policy. We took the motion's arguments seriously — all of them — and checked each against the historical record.

OCTOBER 5, 2026 · 8 MIN READ

On October 4, 2026, the Green Party of England and Wales voted — 1,022 to 813, at its annual conference in Brighton — to declare Zionism a form of racism. The motion, now formal party policy, calls for Zionism to be "treated as any other form of racism" and demands a single Palestinian state "in place of Israel." It is the first time a mainstream European party has adopted such a position.

The claim deserves something rarer than outrage: examination. So let's take the motion's arguments seriously — state each one fairly, in its proponents' own terms — and check it against the historical record. Documents, not opinions.

I. "Zionism is built on theft"

The claim

In interviews around the conference, proponents put it plainly: Zionism is racism because the state it built rests on stolen land. As one Greens for Palestine spokesperson told the Spotlight newspaper, Zionism means "the demolishing of our homes, of our land." The theft is the original sin; everything else follows from it.

The record

Theft has a specific meaning: taking what belongs to someone else, without consent and without compensation. The history of Jewish land acquisition does not fit it:

  • Under Ottoman rule and the British Mandate, Jewish buyers purchased land — overwhelmingly from absentee Arab landowners, and routinely at above-market prices. In the 1930s, 52.6% of Jewish-purchased land was bought from non-Palestinian landowners and 24.6% from Palestinian ones. In a documented 1929 purchase at Emek Hefer, the Jewish National Fund's agent privately agreed to pay the selling family four pounds per dunam — four times the public auction price.
  • Buyers deliberately sought sparsely populated land — the coastal plain, the Jezreel Valley — where few tenants would be displaced.
  • When the world weighed in, the UN General Assembly voted 33 to 13 on November 29, 1947 (Resolution 181) to partition the land into a Jewish state and an Arab state. The Jewish leadership accepted. The Arab leadership rejected it outright.
  • On May 15, 1948, the day the British Mandate expired, five Arab armies invaded the new Jewish state.

Purchase is not theft. A partition plan voted by the world and accepted by one side is not theft. Territory retained after a defensive war — launched in rejection of that plan — may be debated on many grounds, but "theft" is not an honest word for it. Words have meanings; the record doesn't fit this one.

II. "Zionism privileges one ethnic group — it is apartheid"

The claim

This is the motion's central charge: Zionism "privileges one ethnic group on the basis of the subjugation of others." Israel, in this account, is an apartheid state, and Zionism is the racism that built it.

The record

Apartheid was not a metaphor. It was a legal architecture: the Black majority could not vote, could not stand for parliament, could not sit as judges. Apply that test:

  • About 21% of Israel's citizens are Arab. They vote in every election — Arab parties have held Knesset seats since the first elections in 1949. The Joint List won 15 seats in 2015; in 2021, the United Arab List became the first Arab party to join a governing coalition.
  • Arab citizens serve as judges at every level. In 2022, Khaled Kabub became the first Muslim justice permanently appointed to the Supreme Court.
  • Weeks ago, when Israel's elections committee moved to disqualify Arab parties from the ballot, the Supreme Court unanimously overturned the ban.

None of this claims perfection — Israel's democracy is loudly contested, including by its own Arab citizens. That contest is itself the point: a state where the allegedly subjugated minority votes, legislates, judges, and defeats the majority's institutions in court has failed the apartheid test at every step. Policies can be argued. But calling the national movement itself racist requires the word to do work the facts won't support.

III. "Even the United Nations said so"

The claim

UN Resolution 3379 (1975) determined that "Zionism is a form of racism and racial discrimination." The Greens are reviving an established international position.

The record

Resolution 3379 passed 72 to 35 on November 10, 1975 — sponsored by the Arab League, carried by the Soviet bloc and its allies at the Cold War's height. America's ambassador, Daniel Patrick Moynihan, warned the chamber it was about to "make anti-Semitism international law."

Then the Cold War ended. On December 16, 1991, the General Assembly revoked the determination with Resolution 46/86 — a single sentence: "The General Assembly decides to revoke the determination contained in its resolution 3379." The vote was 111 to 25. President George H. W. Bush personally introduced the repeal, and Israel had made revocation a condition of joining the Madrid Peace Conference. Ninety of the countries that had voted for the original resolution voted to repeal it.

A claim the United Nations itself repudiated — by a larger majority than the one that adopted it — is not a precedent. It is a retraction.

IV. "This is about an ideology, not a people"

The claim

The motion is careful here: it targets "the political ideology and practice of Zionism," it says, not Jewish people. "Not all Jews are Zionists and not all Zionists are Jews."

The record

At the same conference, an amendment was proposed to ensure the motion could not be used to "pressure, proscribe or discipline" members for belonging to synagogues or Jewish institutions that define themselves as Zionist. It failed — 684 in favor, 1,026 against. The party voted down, by a wide margin, the one clause separating the ideology from the people.

And the distinction was thin to begin with. Zionism — the belief that the Jewish people, like other peoples, are entitled to self-determination in their historic homeland — is held by most British Jews and Jewish institutions; the Board of Deputies warned the motion brands them "racist." Declare that belief racist, then vote down protections for the Jews who hold it, and the motion has answered its own distinction.

What happens to the word

There is a pattern here worth noticing. In 1975, "Zionism is racism" was a Cold War maneuver — passed by an automatic majority, repealed when the Cold War ended. In 2026 it returns as party policy, with the same architecture: redefine a national movement as a racial crime, then present the redefinition as moral clarity.

But words are tools, and tools wear out with misuse. "Racism" names something real and terrible — the belief that some human beings are worth less because of their blood. Stretch it to cover a movement that purchased its land, accepted partition, enfranchised its minorities, and saw the charge itself repealed by the body that made it, and the word doesn't grow bigger. It grows emptier. And when the real thing comes, we will find we have spent the word that was meant to name it.

The record is there for anyone who wants it.
