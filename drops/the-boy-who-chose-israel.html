The Boy Who Chose Israel

High school at eight, a quantum physics PhD at fifteen, a Nature paper at sixteen. Laurent Simons could walk into any laboratory on earth. The Belgian prodigy is expected at Bar-Ilan — because that is where he judges the science to be.

OCTOBER 20, 2026 · 4 MIN READ

Most sixteen-year-olds are revising for their school-leaving exams. Laurent Simons has already finished school (at eight), a bachelor's degree (at eleven), a master's (at twelve), and a doctorate in quantum physics (at fifteen). This year his name appeared among the co-authors of a paper in Nature. Now he is packing for his next move: the research laboratories of Bar-Ilan University, in Israel.

He could have gone anywhere. That is precisely the point.

I. The résumé

Born in Ostend, Belgium, Simons finished primary school at about six and graduated from high school at eight. He began electrical engineering at Eindhoven University of Technology at nine; when that path stalled in 2019, he switched to physics at the University of Antwerp and simply accelerated — bachelor's at eleven, master's at twelve, then a doctorate in quantum physics at fifteen, with a thesis on Bose polarons, among the most abstract corners of the field. He is now pursuing a second PhD, in medical science.

II. The mission

The speed has a reason, and it is not ambition for its own sake. Simons lost both his grandparents when he was eleven, and from that point he has stated his goal plainly: to extend human life — to help people live longer, healthier lives. His first doctorate gave him the deepest toolkit physics offers. The second, in medicine, aims it at the body. The quantum physicist is retraining as a longevity researcher because the clock he is racing is not his own career. It is everyone's.

III. The choice

Simons has kept a longstanding relationship with the Israeli Embassy in Belgium, and last week he was hosted at the home of Ambassador David Saranga. Afterwards Saranga wrote:

“Contrary to the impression that has been created, Israel has many friends in Belgium. They come from all parts of society, different religions, backgrounds and age groups. One of them is Laurent Simons.”

According to Saranga, Simons chose Israel for the standard of its science in the fields that interest him — despite neither he nor his parents having any connection to Judaism. “It's a combination of genuine love for Israel and admiration for scientific research,” the ambassador explained. Simons is expected to join Bar-Ilan's research labs; the precise framework has yet to be announced.

Consider the moment. Across Europe and America, campaigns press universities to sever ties with Israeli institutions — to make Israel a pariah of the laboratory. Into that moment walks the most sought-after young scientist on the continent, a teenager courted by universities and tech companies alike, and he chooses the pariah. Not out of ancestry, not out of obligation — out of respect for the work.

Voting with his feet

Committees pass resolutions about Israel. Boycotters draft letters about Israel. Laurent Simons, who has no dog in the fight and every option on the table, simply looked at where the science was strongest and went there.

Resolutions are cheap. A choice like his is not.
