Death for a Deleted Comment

Sivarasa Anojan, 24, deleted his Facebook comment and filmed an apology. A Saudi court gave him five years and an $800,000 fine. The prosecution appealed — and the sentence became death. This is a story about one man. It is also a story about who gets to be outraged.

OCTOBER 6, 2026 · 6 MIN READ

He deleted the comment. He filmed an apology. It did not matter. Sivarasa Anojan, a 24-year-old migrant worker from Sri Lanka, is facing execution in Saudi Arabia — for a Facebook comment he took down within hours of posting it.

This is not an allegation buried on social media. Sri Lanka's president is pursuing clemency. Its foreign minister has briefed parliament. A five-member legal delegation is flying to Riyadh today to appeal to the Saudi Supreme Court. And yet, outside Sri Lanka and parts of India, the story has barely registered — certainly not with the human-rights machinery that can summon emergency sessions, campus encampments, and global boycott campaigns on shorter notice.

I. The man

Anojan is a Sri Lankan Tamil from Narpittimunai, in the Kalmunai region of the Eastern Province. He traveled to Saudi Arabia for work in September 2025, employed as a quantity surveyor. He was 24 years old — one of the millions of South Asian migrant workers whose labor keeps the Gulf running, and whose legal protections there are paper-thin.

On July 2nd, 2026, he was arrested. The charge: a Facebook comment deemed derogatory toward Islam and the Prophet Muhammad.

II. The comment

The full context matters, and it makes the case more disturbing, not less. According to Sri Lankan reporting, the episode began when derogatory remarks targeting Hindu beliefs and deities were circulated in Tamil on social media, following an incident during a Hindu pilgrimage in Sri Lanka. Anojan — a Hindu — responded in kind. His comment was captured, reported to the Saudi authorities, and became a criminal case.

He deleted the comment. He posted a video apologizing. Under the logic of any justice system that recognizes remorse, the matter might have ended there. It did not.

III. The escalation

On July 15th, the Al-Ahsa Criminal Court convicted Anojan under Saudi Arabia's Anti-Cyber Crime Law and handed down the maximum penalty that law allows: five years in prison and a fine of three million Saudi riyals — roughly $800,000, a sum a migrant quantity surveyor could never pay. A severe sentence for a deleted comment. But it was not severe enough for the Saudi prosecution.

Prosecutors appealed, demanding the case be retried under Sharia law as blasphemy. The appellate court agreed — and replaced the prison term with a death sentence.

Read that sequence again. The state did not merely punish a man for words he retracted and apologized for. It looked at five years and an $800,000 fine and decided the deficiency was leniency.

IV. The rescue effort

To its credit, Sri Lanka is fighting. President Anura Kumara Dissanayake has pursued clemency. Foreign Minister Vijitha Herath told parliament the prosecution's appeal was followed by the capital sentence, and the foreign ministry says it is working through its embassy in Riyadh. Today, a delegation including parliamentarian Faiszer Musthapha, President's Counsel Upul Kumarapperuma, and an official from the Attorney General's department is traveling to Saudi Arabia to prepare an appeal to the Saudi Supreme Court.

Across Sri Lanka there have been signature campaigns, candlelight vigils, and protests. In Chennai, the Hindu Munnani petitioned the Sri Lankan Deputy High Commission and urged India's government to intervene. Anojan is reportedly the seventh Sri Lankan to be sentenced to death by Saudi courts.

It may not be enough. It was not enough for Rizana Nafeek, the Sri Lankan domestic worker executed in 2013 despite an eight-year campaign by human-rights organizations and the Sri Lankan government. The pattern is the point.

V. The question

Now the uncomfortable part — the reason this story belongs here.

There exists an international human-rights industry with budgets in the hundreds of millions, with the power to convene emergency sessions, to fill campuses, to make a country's name synonymous with atrocity within a news cycle. It has demonstrated, repeatedly, that it can mobilize planetary outrage at speed. A man is about to be executed for a deleted Facebook comment — after apologizing — and that machinery is producing barely a murmur.

Be precise about the claim. It is not that no one has ever criticized Saudi Arabia's death penalty; some organizations do, in reports few read. The claim is about proportion. Outrage is a finite resource, and how it is allocated reveals what it is for. When the same institutions that declare some conflicts the moral emergency of our time cannot raise their voice above a whisper for a 24-year-old facing the sword for a retracted comment, the selectivity itself becomes the story.

A note on fairness. This piece does not claim human-rights organizations have said nothing about Saudi executions — several document them. The argument is narrower and, we think, harder to dismiss: that the scale of mobilization bears no relation to the scale of the abuse, and that the gap tracks politics, not principle. If the evidence showed otherwise — sustained, proportionate campaigns for cases like Anojan's — we would print that instead.

Sivarasa Anojan deleted his comment. He apologized on camera. A court gave him five years and a fine he could never pay, and the state appealed for his death — and got it. His government is flying lawyers to Riyadh today to try to save his life.

The question this publication keeps asking is simple: who is watching, and who decided this wasn't worth watching? The record is there for anyone who wants it.
