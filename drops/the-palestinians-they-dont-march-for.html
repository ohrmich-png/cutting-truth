The Palestinians They Don't March For

Amin Abed — the Gazan dissident Hamas beat with hammers for opposing them — has a program for Gaza: disarm Hamas, deradicalize the young, melt every weapon into a garbage container. The movement that claims to care about Palestinians has never heard his name.

OCTOBER 11, 2026 · 5 MIN READ

There is a man in Gaza who has paid for his opinions with his arms, his legs, his teeth, and his skull. His name is Amin Abed. In 2019 he helped start the “We Want to Live” movement against Hamas's rule. Hamas answered with interrogations, then with hammers. This week, in a 76-second video, he laid out his program for Gaza's future: disarm Hamas, deradicalize the young, and melt every seized weapon into garbage containers — so they will finally be of some use to ordinary Gazans.

The people who march for Palestine in London and New York have never heard of him. That is the story.

I. The man

Abed's record is documented, not rumored. In March 2019, Gazans took to the streets against Hamas's taxes and economic stranglehold under the banner “We Want to Live” — بدنا نعيش. Abed helped organize. That August, Hamas's internal security summoned him and interrogated him over the movement and his social media posts. He kept posting.

In July 2024, some twenty masked men dragged him off a street in northern Gaza, announced themselves as internal security, and beat him for half an hour with hammers and the butts of their weapons. They broke his arms, his legs, his fingers, his teeth — a rock to the mouth — and fractured his skull. He was carried to a hospital in critical condition. From his hospital bed, in a video published by Al-Arabiya, he addressed Hamas directly: he had expected them to kill him, he said — but you were wrong not to kill me. As long as his tongue speaks, he said, he will keep speaking.

He is still speaking. That is what makes him dangerous — to Hamas, and to a certain narrative.

II. The program

The video, posted this week, is Abed at his most direct. Hamas, he says, must be disarmed — up to this moment it has refused every demand to give up its weapons, weapons which, he notes, did not protect the Gaza Strip. He calls on Palestinians of all walks of life to take part in collecting them.

But then he goes further than the diplomats. “The problem will not end simply by disarming Hamas,” he says. What Gaza needs, in his words, is a deradicalization program — a confrontation with the extremist ideology that, as he puts it, has clouded minds for a hundred years and more, reaching even people who are not Hamas supporters. It cannot be confronted, he says, except with a humane, inclusive, national way of thinking: building a national and humanistic mindset to face down the extremist one.

And then the line that will follow him: his personal proposal is to collect Hamas's weapons and recycle them — melt them down into garbage containers. So that they will finally be of some use and benefit to regular Gazans.

Read that twice. A man Hamas crippled is not asking for revenge. He is asking for sanitation.

III. The hypocrisy

Now consider the self-described friends of Palestine. For two years they have filled the streets of Western capitals, occupied campuses, and chanted about Gaza. Ask them to name one Gazan dissident — one Palestinian who risked everything to oppose Hamas — and you will get silence. Abed's name means nothing to them. The “We Want to Live” movement means nothing to them. The anti-Hamas protests that erupted in Gaza itself in March 2025 — ordinary Gazans chanting against the terror regime while the war still raged — earned a fraction of the attention given to a single campus encampment.

This is not an accident of news cycles. It is a selection. The movement that claims to care about Palestinians has no room for the Palestinians who say the thing it cannot hear: that Hamas is the catastrophe, that the ideology must be uprooted, that the weapons should become garbage cans. Abed's program is the actual Palestinian position on what Gaza needs — disarmament, deradicalization, a humane national future — and it is invisible to the people waving his flag.

They do not march for Abed because Abed indicts them. His existence proves that the suffering of Gazans has an author in Gaza itself — and that the people who claim to weep for Palestine cannot even name the Palestinians Hamas tortures.

IV. The meaning

There is a simple test for anyone who says they care about Palestinians: do they listen to Palestinians? Not to the spokesmen, not to the slogans — to the man with the broken hands who keeps talking anyway.

Abed is not asking the world to bomb anyone. He is asking for the weapons to be taken away and melted into something useful. It is the most modest proposal imaginable from a man owed the most immodest revenge. The people who march past his story on their way to their next rally should ask themselves why a Gazan's voice only counts when it says what they already believe.

The test

Every cause has a moment when it must choose between its narrative and its people. The pro-Palestinian movement chose long ago: the narrative. That is why Amin Abed — Palestinian, Gazan, tortured by Hamas for the crime of wanting to live — is a stranger to the crowds carrying Palestinian flags.

He will keep talking anyway. Broken hands and all. The question is whether anyone who claims to care is listening.
