An Allée in Paris

On Tuesday, the city of Paris inaugurated a park alley bearing the names of Shiri, Ariel and Kfir Bibas — the first permanent tribute to the family anywhere in Europe's public space. Three years after October 7th, their names are now written into the map of Paris.

OCTOBER 7, 2026 · 4 MIN READ

There is a path in the Parc Monceau that did not exist on any map a week ago. It is called the Allée Ariel, Kfir et Shiri Bibas — and it is the first time the family's names have been written permanently into the public space of a European capital.

The inauguration took place on Tuesday, October 6th, at La Rotonde in the Parc Monceau, in Paris's 8th arrondissement. The mayor of Paris, Emmanuel Grégoire, presided over the ceremony, in the presence of members of the Bibas family, representatives of the Paris Council and the French Jewish community.

I. The ceremony

The alley — unveiled at 12:45 at the park's Rotonde, on avenue Ferdousi — will bear the names of all three: Ariel, Kfir and Shiri Bibas. Among those present were the president of CRIF, Yonathan Arfi; the mayor of the 8th arrondissement, Catherine Lecuyer; and Sandra Ifrah, spokesperson for the association Woman United for Peace.

Paris does not hand out its street names lightly. Every plaque in the city is a decision about what — and who — deserves to be remembered by strangers a century from now. On Tuesday, the city made that decision for a mother and her two small boys.

II. The names

Shiri Bibas and her two sons — Ariel, four years old, and Kfir, nine months old — were seized from their home in Kibbutz Nir Oz during the Hamas attack of October 7th, 2023. Kfir was the youngest hostage held in the Gaza Strip. All three were murdered in captivity.

In the months that followed, their red hair became emblematic. The two small boys — one a toddler, one a baby — became the faces the world could not look away from, and enduring symbols of the tragedy of the abducted and their families.

III. The first

What makes Tuesday's inauguration historic is its permanence. This is the first permanent homage to the Bibas family in European public space — not a march, not a poster campaign, but a name on the map of Paris.

There is a particular weight to that. Marches disperse. Posters fade. A name carved into the geography of a city is a promise made to people not yet born: when they walk here, they will ask who these names belonged to — and someone will tell them.

The alley

Three years after the morning everything changed, a path in a Paris park carries three names. Children will walk it on their way to the playground. Runners will pass it at dawn. And the city, without a speech or a slogan, will have said what it means: we remember them here.

That is what a permanent tribute does. It doesn't argue. It simply refuses to forget.
