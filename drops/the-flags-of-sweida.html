The Flags of Sweida

In southern Syria, the Druze — massacred in July 2025, abandoned by Damascus — held an October 7th memorial with Israeli flags and photographs of Netanyahu. Their spiritual leader calls them “an integral part of Israel.” This is the third such demonstration in fourteen months.

OCTOBER 8, 2026 · 5 MIN READ

A few kilometers across the border, in the Druze-majority city of Sweida in southern Syria, they held a memorial for October 7th this week. They waved Israeli flags. They held photographs of Benjamin Netanyahu. They read out statements of support for Israel.

This was not the first time. It was the third.

I. The memorial

On Wednesday, October 7th — the third anniversary of the massacre — Druze residents gathered in the center of a-Suwayda with Israeli flags, photographs of Israel's prime minister, and messages condemning terror, alongside tributes to those murdered in the war. Banners spoke of the shared suffering of Druze and Israelis at the hands of Arab regimes in the region. A convoy rolled through the city in a public show of support for Israel from inside Syrian territory — a rarity in the Arab world.

Among the photographs they held up were those of the twelve Druze children murdered when a Hezbollah rocket struck a soccer field in Majdal Shams on July 27th, 2024. The children of the Golan and the children of Sweida, remembered together, under the same flag.

II. The road here

To understand what an Israeli flag means in a Syrian street, rewind fourteen months. In mid-July 2025, clashes erupted in Sweida between Druze militias and local Sunni Bedouin tribes. Government forces intervened claiming to restore order — and effectively sided with the Bedouins against the Druze. What followed were massacres: executions, house burnings, kidnappings. The Syrian Observatory for Human Rights counts more than 2,000 dead, including 789 Druze civilians; a Syrian government committee documented 1,760. The figures differ. The horror does not.

Israel intervened — dozens of airstrikes on government convoys, and a strike on the Syrian Defense Ministry headquarters in Damascus — stating it acted to protect the Druze and demilitarize southern Syria. A month later, in August 2025, hundreds marched in Sweida and nearby towns demanding the right to self-determination: the largest protests since the clashes, and the first ever held under that slogan. Some waved Israeli flags, the Associated Press reported, to thank Israel for intervening on their side.

III. The declaration

Then, this August, came the words. Following a march in Suwayda whose participants carried Israeli flags and pictures of Netanyahu — organizers said it expressed “support and gratitude” to Israel — Sheikh Hikmat al-Hijri, the Druze spiritual leader of the province, declared that the Druze “are an integral part of the State of Israel.”

“We found no one but Western countries, especially and primarily the State of Israel and the United States, to embrace us,” he said. Druze in Syria and Israel, he added, are “one family” — and Israel, he said, “is truly a state of institutions, a state of knowledge and civilization… where the law is the supreme authority for everyone.” He called the July 2025 events a “war of extermination” and tied Suwayda's future to “full sovereign rights and the right to self-determination.”

A note of honesty: al-Hijri leads one camp of Syria's Druze spiritual leadership, not all of it. His pro-Israel line is his camp's position. But it is the camp holding the street.

IV. The meaning

Step back and look at what is happening. A religious minority inside Syria — a country that spent decades defining itself by hostility to Israel — is publicly choosing Israel: its flag, its prime minister's portrait, its model of a state. Not in secret. In the streets, on camera, three times in fourteen months.

Minorities in the Middle East have long memories, and they keep a ledger. The Druze kept theirs: who massacred them in Sweida, who bombed the convoys coming to finish the job, and who held up photographs of twelve dead children on a soccer field. The ledger balanced toward Jerusalem.

The region's map is being redrawn — not by treaties this time, but by communities deciding who protected them when it mattered. Watch the flags. They are telling you the future before the diplomats do.

The flag

There is a photograph from Sweida this week: a sea of people, Israeli flags rising above Druze ones, a child's sign in Arabic. Two years ago, waving that flag in that street would have been a death sentence. This week it was a memorial.

That is what protection buys. Not gratitude as a sentiment — gratitude as a political fact, waved in daylight, in Syria, for the cameras.
